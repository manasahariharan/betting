How Crowds Get Probability Wrong

We analyzed 806 resolved Polymarket markets to test whether prediction market prices behave like well-calibrated probabilities—and where they break down.

Part 1

What Are Prediction Markets Actually Predicting?

A prediction market puts a price on the future. If a market says an event has a 72% chance of happening, that price should—in theory—mean roughly 72 out of 100 similar events would actually occur.

But do these prices actually mean what they claim? Can a number on a trading screen be trusted as a genuine probability?

Market Timeline
How a single market probability evolves toward resolution

A market opens

In early 2024, Polymarket opened a question: "Will the Fed cut interest rates before January 2025?"

The opening price: 48%. The crowd thought a rate cut was roughly a coin flip.

Probabilities fluctuate

Through spring, sticky inflation data pushed the price down to 35%. Through summer it bounced back as the jobs market cooled.

This is the market updating in real time as new information arrives—CPI prints, jobs reports, Fed speeches.

Then a defining event

After August's CPI print confirmed cooling inflation, the price climbed sharply to 95%. The Fed cut in September. The market resolved YES.

The crowd absorbed an event and reweighted its probability accordingly.

The question this raises

From 48% at open to 100% at resolution—the crowd moved a long way. But was its midflight pricing actually right?

When Polymarket says “72% chance,” does that really happen 72% of the time? This is the question of calibration—and it's what we set out to test across 806 markets.

Part 2

How Do Market Probabilities Behave?

A calibration chart answers the question directly. We group markets by their predicted probability, then check how often those events actually happened.

If the crowd is perfectly calibrated, every point falls on the diagonal line—events priced at 30% happen 30% of the time, events priced at 80% happen 80% of the time.

Calibration Chart
Predicted probability vs. observed outcome rate

Overall: reasonably well-calibrated

Across all 806 markets, Polymarket prices track actual outcomes fairly well. The Brier Skill Score is BSS 0.62, meaning the crowd outperforms a naive baseline by 62%.

But the curve hints at something interesting in the middle range…

Early in a market's life: noisy and uncertain

At 10% of market lifetime elapsed, calibration is rougher. BSS 0.46—still better than guessing, but prices bounce around the diagonal with less precision.

Information is sparse early on. The crowd is making its best guess with limited data.

Midlife: improving, but mid-range prices are compressed

At 50% elapsed, BSS 0.58. The curve tightens at the extremes but shows compression in the 30–60% range—prices cluster around 50% even when outcomes diverge.

This pattern is consistent with underconfidence: the crowd hedges toward the middle.

Near resolution: sharp and decisive

At 90% elapsed, BSS 0.68. The calibration curve snaps to the diagonal. Markets are pulling apart—winners go high, losers go low.

By the end, prices converge on truth. The crowd figures it out—eventually.

Final prices: the best the crowd ever gets

Using the last available trading price, BSS 0.73. The curve hugs the diagonal closely.

But this number is partly inflated—by the time of the final price, resolution information has often leaked into the market. The dynamics tell the more honest story.

When Does the Crowd Get It Right?

Brier Skill Score over each market's relative lifetime. The crowd steadily improves—but the pace varies dramatically by category.

Sports markets gain almost no accuracy over their lifetime (BSS +0.08 from 10% to 95% elapsed). Part 3

Where Were Markets Most Surprised?

Some predictions weren't just wrong—they were confidently wrong. These are the markets where the crowd's price diverged most dramatically from reality.

The longshot pattern

Of the 420 markets priced at 15% or below, the crowd overpriced them by an average of 1.3 percentage points. That sounds tiny, but at these low probabilities it means events were priced at roughly double their actual occurrence rate.

High-volume markets show almost no overpricing (-0.03 pp), suggesting informed money corrects the bias. Low-volume markets overprice longshots by 3.1 pp—where attention is thin, mispricing persists.

The biggest surprises

These are the markets where the final trading price was farthest from the actual outcome—the crowd's most confident mistakes and most dramatic upsets.

Methodology & data notes

Sample: 1,000 resolved binary markets drawn via stratified sampling (proportional to category) from ~10,000 Polymarket markets with volume ≥ $10k. 806 markets had valid CLOB price history and are included in calibration analysis. 512 markets met the additional duration (≥ 5 days) and history observation (≥ 4 ticks) filters for dynamics analysis.

Price source: CLOB /prices-history endpoint with interval=max, fidelity=720 (12-hour intervals). The second-to-last price tick is used as the forecast price to avoid settlement contamination of the final tick. Never uses outcomePrices from the Gamma API as forecast prices—those reflect post-settlement values, not predictions.

Brier Skill Score: BSS = 1 - BS / Var(resolved_yes). Not strictly proper as a skill score; asymptotically proper with large samples (Murphy 1973). Per-snapshot BSS denominators differ slightly because Var(y) is computed on the markets that have a price at that snapshot.

Log-odds slope: Logistic regression of resolved_yes on log(p/(1-p)); prices clipped to [0.01, 0.99]; unpenalised MLE via LogisticRegression(C=1e9). Slope = 1.0 is perfect calibration; > 1.0 is underconfident; < 1.0 is overconfident. Slope is reported as null when minority-class share < 5% because the MLE diverges (near-separation).

Bootstrap CIs: 500 resamples, market-level, seed=42. CIs assume market-level independence and may understate uncertainty due to correlated event structure.

Category source: Market tags via /markets?include_tag=true (not the category field, which is null on ~83% of markets).

Dynamics: Relative-time normalisation standardises chronology, not information arrival. Late-snapshot (90%+) log-odds slopes may diverge due to near-separation as winners and losers pull apart; BSS is the more reliable dynamics signal at those time points.

Explore-by-category section: Curves and BSS are read from the dynamics sample (512 markets that cleared the ≥ 5 day duration and ≥ 4 tick history filters), so the per-category numbers here will differ slightly from the 806-market overall calibration view in the scrolly above. The market list shows all 512 markets sortable by close date, search-filtered by question text. A market's overlay dot uses its most-recent CLOB tick at or before the selected snapshot; if a market has no tick before that point (mainly an issue at the 10% snapshot), the dot falls back to the nearest earlier snapshot and is rendered semi-transparent with a tooltip note.

Limitations: Market prices are not IID forecasts from a stable process. Aggregate calibration curves are mixtures of heterogeneous mechanisms. Final prices may partly reflect resolution certainty rather than forecasting quality. Observed pricing patterns are consistent with reported interpretations but do not prove specific behavioral mechanisms.
