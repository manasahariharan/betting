How Crowds Get Probability Wrong

We analyzed 1,998 resolved Polymarket markets from the last 30 days to test whether prediction market prices behave like well-calibrated probabilities— and where they break down.

Scroll to explore ↓
Part 1

What Are Prediction Markets Actually Predicting?

A prediction market puts a price on the future. If a market says an event has a 72% chance of happening, that price should—in theory—mean roughly 72 out of 100 similar events would actually occur.

But do these prices actually mean what they claim? Can a number on a trading screen be trusted as a genuine probability?

Market Timeline
How a single market probability evolves toward resolution

A market opens

In early 2024, Polymarket opened a question: "Will the Fed cut interest rates before January 2025?"

The opening price: 48%. The crowd thought a rate cut was roughly a coin flip.

Probabilities fluctuate

Through spring, sticky inflation data pushed the price down to 35%. Through summer it bounced back as the jobs market cooled.

This is the market updating in real time as new information arrives—CPI prints, jobs reports, Fed speeches.

Then a defining event

After August's CPI print confirmed cooling inflation, the price climbed sharply to 95%. The Fed cut in September. The market resolved YES.

The crowd absorbed an event and reweighted its probability accordingly.

The question this raises

From 48% at open to 100% at resolution—the crowd moved a long way. But was its midflight pricing actually right?

When Polymarket says “72% chance,” does that really happen 72% of the time? This is the question of calibration—and it's what we set out to test across nearly 2,000 recently-resolved markets.

Part 2

How Do Market Probabilities Behave?

A calibration chart answers the question directly. We group markets by their predicted probability, then check how often those events actually happened.

If the crowd is perfectly calibrated, every point falls on the diagonal line—events priced at 30% happen 30% of the time, events priced at 80% happen 80% of the time.

Calibration Chart
Predicted probability vs. observed outcome rate

Overall: only marginally better than chance

Across all 1,998 markets in our 30-day window, Polymarket prices beat a naive base-rate baseline by just BSS 0.18. The curve hugs the diagonal at the extremes but smears badly through the middle.

This is a much weaker signal than a politics-heavy historical sample would show—the recent window is dominated by short, sports- and crypto-driven micro-markets that resolve close to a 50/50 base rate.

Early in a market's life: even noisier

At 10% of market lifetime elapsed, BSS 0.14—barely above the base-rate baseline. Many markets haven't even started trading actively yet.

Information is sparse early on. The crowd is making its best guess with limited data.

Midlife: tails are inward of the diagonal

At 50% elapsed, BSS 0.18. The log-odds calibration slope is 0.87—below 1. The crowd's extreme prices are mildly too extreme: longshots resolve YES a little more often than the price suggested, favorites a little less often.

This is the opposite direction of the classic underconfidence story—a sign that short-duration markets are more prone to mild overconfidence at the tails.

Near resolution: improving, but only slightly

At 90% elapsed, BSS 0.21. The curve sharpens a bit, but the gain across the whole lifetime is only +0.08 BSS—a much flatter trajectory than the politics-heavy historical samples show.

Most of these markets are short-duration; there's just not much time for new information to arrive.

Final prices: the best the crowd ever gets

Using the last available trading price, BSS 0.19. That's lower than the 95%-snapshot BSS (0.22)—the very last tick is settlement-contaminated for some markets and actually hurts calibration when averaged across the dynamics-eligible 783 markets.

The dynamics below tell a more honest story than any single snapshot.

The dynamics trajectory

When Does the Crowd Get It Right?

Brier Skill Score over each market's relative lifetime. The overall trajectory is shallow—but the per-category gap is what drives it.

Sports markets dominate the sample (708 of 783 dynamics-eligible markets) and barely improve over their lifetime (BSS 0.07 → 0.15, just +0.08). Game outcomes arrive at game time, not gradually—the relative-time normalisation standardises chronology, not information arrival. Longer-duration crypto markets (52 markets in the dynamics sample) tell a very different story: BSS climbs from 0.67 to 0.96 across lifetime. These are typically “Will X hit $Y by date” markets where the reference price gradually reveals itself. Politics and Science have too few dynamics-eligible markets in this 30-day window (19 and 4 respectively, below the n ≥ 20 threshold) to fit a stable trajectory.

Part 3

Where Were Markets Most Surprised?

Some predictions weren't just wrong—they were confidently wrong. These are the markets where the crowd's price diverged most dramatically from reality.

The longshot pattern

Of the 274 markets priced at 15% or below, only 14 resolved YES (5.1%)—but the crowd's prices were a little too low on average, by -1.5 percentage points. Longshots aren't being overpriced in this 30-day sample; if anything, they're slightly underpriced.

The 85 favorites (priced ≥ 85%) go the other way: the crowd is overpricing them by +2.7 pp. Combined with the longshot direction, this is the signature of mild overconfidence at the tails—the calibration slope is 0.88 with a CI that just barely crosses 1 ([0.77, 0.99]).

Volume splits the pattern further: low-volume markets are essentially flat (-1.0 pp longshot bias), high-volume markets actually underprice longshots more aggressively (-2.9 pp). Where there's heavier trading activity in this sample, the dollars are pushing crowd prices below the eventual realisation rate.

The biggest surprises

These are the markets where the final trading price was farthest from the actual outcome. In this 30-day window they're almost entirely weather and temperature micro-markets—the crowd's most confident mistakes are about whether the high in Beijing or Atlanta will land in a narrow band on a given day.

Methodology & data notes

Sample: 2,000 resolved binary markets drawn via stratified sampling (proportional to category) from a recency-windowed universe of 2,325 qualifying markets that closed in the last 30 days (the latest data we could pull from the Gamma API at run time). The fetcher scanned 20,000 raw markets sorted by endDate descending with end_date_min/end_date_max filters; client-side filtering kept binary outcomes with volume ≥ $10k that resolved to a clear YES/NO. 1,998 of the 2,000 sampled markets had a usable CLOB final price. 783 markets cleared the additional duration (≥ 5 days) and history observation (≥ 4 ticks) filters for dynamics analysis.

Sample composition: Sports 50%, Crypto 39%, Science 10%, Politics 1.5%. Business and Pop Culture are absent from the 30-day window. This is a fundamentally different population from a long-history Polymarket sample (which would be ~40% politics); many of the calibration findings here reflect short-duration sports and crypto micro-markets more than long-running political contests.

Price source: CLOB /prices-history endpoint with interval=max, fidelity=720 (12-hour intervals). The second-to-last price tick is used as the forecast price to avoid settlement contamination of the final tick. Never uses outcomePrices from the Gamma API as forecast prices—those reflect post-settlement values, not predictions.

Brier Skill Score: BSS = 1 - BS / Var(resolved_yes). Not strictly proper as a skill score; asymptotically proper with large samples (Murphy 1973). Per-snapshot BSS denominators differ slightly because Var(y) is computed on the markets that have a price at that snapshot.

Log-odds slope: Logistic regression of resolved_yes on log(p/(1-p)); prices clipped to [0.01, 0.99]; unpenalised MLE via LogisticRegression(C=1e9). Slope = 1.0 is perfect calibration; > 1.0 is underconfident; < 1.0 is overconfident. Slope is reported as null when minority-class share < 5% because the MLE diverges (near-separation).

Bootstrap CIs: 500 resamples, market-level, seed=42. CIs assume market-level independence and may understate uncertainty due to correlated event structure.

Category source: Market tags via /markets?include_tag=true (not the category field, which is null on ~83% of markets).

Dynamics: Relative-time normalisation standardises chronology, not information arrival. Late-snapshot (90%+) log-odds slopes may diverge due to near-separation as winners and losers pull apart; BSS is the more reliable dynamics signal at those time points.

Interactive scrolly step + per-market journey: The dial + category controls at the end of the scrolly drive the same sticky calibration chart that the walk-through animates, reading from the dynamics sample (783 markets that cleared the ≥ 5 day duration and ≥ 4 tick history filters). The per-category numbers there will differ from the 1,998-market overall calibration view in the scrolly because the duration filter strips out almost all short-duration sports/crypto micro-markets. Below the scrolly, the picker covers all 783 markets sortable by close date with a search box. Selecting a market draws its YES price across the 7 snapshots (10/25/50/75/90/95/Final) as a journey chart, with a dashed reference at the resolved outcome (0% if NO, 100% if YES) and the shaded area showing the gap. Snapshots with no CLOB tick (mostly an issue at 10%) are gapped on the line rather than interpolated.

Limitations: Market prices are not IID forecasts from a stable process. Aggregate calibration curves are mixtures of heterogeneous mechanisms. Final prices may partly reflect resolution certainty rather than forecasting quality. Observed pricing patterns are consistent with reported interpretations but do not prove specific behavioral mechanisms.
